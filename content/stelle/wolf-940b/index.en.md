{{% box tipo="definizione" titolo="Wolf 940B at a glance" %}} 
Wolf 940B is a **brown dwarf**[^nanabruna] orbiting a small red star, Wolf 940A, a little over $40$ light-years from the Sun. It was discovered in 2008 and described in a study published in early 2009. It is slightly smaller than Jupiter, but between $20$ and $45$ times more massive, depending on the estimated age. With a temperature of about $600\ \text{K}$, just over $300$ degrees Celsius, it was at the time one of the coldest objects ever observed outside the Solar System, a record it lost in the following years, after the discovery of even colder brown dwarfs.  Its importance, however, lies not only in its low temperature, but in the fact that it is not an isolated object: Wolf 940B was the first brown dwarf this cold whose distance, age, and chemical composition could be reliably determined, thanks to the star it orbits. It thus became a reference object, a sort of standard yardstick against which astronomers test the theoretical models of the coldest atmospheres—those of brown dwarfs and, one day, of the giant planets discovered around other stars.

[^nanabruna]: A **brown dwarf** is an object that forms like a star, from the collapse of a gas cloud, but with a mass too small to ignite stable hydrogen-to-helium fusion in its core, the process that makes main-sequence stars like the Sun shine. For a chemical composition similar to the Sun's, the limit below which fusion does not ignite is around $0.072$ solar masses, or about $75$ times the mass of Jupiter. Deprived of the energy source that powers stars, a brown dwarf cools and fades slowly and indefinitely. The discovery of the first brown dwarf was confirmed in 1995.

## A brown companion for a red dwarf

In the constellation Aquarius, about $12.4$ parsecs[^parsec] from the Sun, lies a small **red dwarf**[^nanarossa] of spectral type **M4**[^classi], too faint to be seen with the naked eye. It was first reported in 1919 by the German astronomer Max Wolf, who was combing through photographic plates of the sky at Heidelberg in search of stars with a high proper motion[^motoproprio], a sign of their closeness to the Sun. For this star he [measured](https://scixplorer.org/abs/1919VeHei...7..195W) a displacement of $0.93''$ per year, very close to today's value. The star has borne his name ever since: **Wolf 940**. It has about a quarter of the Sun's mass and, like the vast majority of stars in the Galaxy, it is entirely ordinary.

[^nanarossa]: **Red dwarfs** are the smallest and coolest stars of the main sequence (the long phase in which stars produce energy by fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores). They belong to spectral class M, have masses between about $0.08$ and $0.6$ solar masses and effective temperatures between about $2\,300$ and $3\,900\ \text{K}$. They are faint, from a few hundredths to less than a thousandth of the Sun's luminosity, which is why hardly any of them is visible to the naked eye. They are, however, the most numerous stars: counting the stars and brown dwarfs within $10$ parsecs of the Sun, Reylé and colleagues [found](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202140985), by analyzing data from the Gaia astrometric satellite, that about $61\%$ are red dwarfs, more than half of which have a spectral type between M3 and M5, like Wolf 940. They burn their nuclear fuel so slowly that they remain on the main sequence far longer than the present age of the universe: according to the models of [Laughlin, Bodenheimer, and Adams](https://doi.org/10.1086/304125), about one and a half trillion years for a red dwarf of $0.2$ solar masses, and as much as ten trillion years for the smallest ones, those close to the limit of $0.08$ solar masses.

[^parsec]: The [parsec]({{< relref "/dizionario/parallasse-trigonometrica-e-parsec/" >}}#calcolare-le-distanze-stellari-in-parsec) is one of the units of distance used in astronomy and corresponds to about $3.26$ light-years. $12.4$ parsecs is therefore a little over $40$ light-years.

[^classi]: Stars and brown dwarfs are arranged in **spectral classes** according to their effective temperature, which can be recognized from the lines and bands present in the [spectrum]({{< relref "/dizionario/spettro-elettromagnetico/" >}}). After the hottest classes (O, B, A, F, G, K) come the red dwarfs of class M and then classes L, T, and Y, introduced from the late 1990s onward for ever colder objects. Classes T and Y consist only of brown dwarfs. A number from $0$ to $9$, placed after the letter, expresses a temperature scale within the class: $0$ indicates the highest temperature, $9$ the lowest. Letter and number together give the **spectral type**, such as M4 or T8; half subtypes, such as T8.5, are also used.

[^motoproprio]: **Proper motion** is the apparent displacement of a star on the celestial sphere, due to its real motion through space relative to the Sun. It is measured in arcseconds (or thousandths of an arcsecond) per year and indicates the transverse displacement of the object across the sky, perpendicular to our line of sight. For the vast majority of stars it is tiny; a large displacement usually indicates a nearby object.

Almost ninety years later, Wolf 940 turned out to have a much smaller and colder companion. The discovery came from **UKIDSS** (*UKIRT Infrared Deep Sky Survey*), a large-scale infrared survey of the sky[^infrarosso] carried out with the *United Kingdom Infrared Telescope* (UKIRT), a $3.8$-meter telescope on the summit of Mauna Kea, in Hawaii. One of the survey's goals was to find the coldest brown dwarfs in the Sun's neighborhood—objects that can hardly be observed in visible light, but that shine faintly in the infrared.

[^infrarosso]: **Infrared** radiation has wavelengths longer than those of red light and is invisible to the human eye. Relatively cold objects emit most of their energy in the infrared. Astronomers divide the near-infrared into bands designated by letters: the most widely used are *Y*, *J*, *H*, and *K*, centered on about $1.0$, $1.25$, $1.65$, and $2.2$ micrometers, respectively.

In a UKIDSS image taken in October 2007, a team led by Ben Burningham, of the University of Hertfordshire, noticed an extremely faint source with infrared colors typical of the coldest T dwarfs. During 2008 the team observed it with a series of telescopes in Chile, the Canary Islands, and Hawaii, and in early 2009 [announced](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2009.14620.x) the discovery. The spectrum obtained with the Gemini North telescope, on Mauna Kea, showed the deep water and methane[^metano] absorption bands that characterize T dwarfs, and made it possible to classify the object as **T8.5**: among the coldest brown dwarfs known at the time.

[^metano]: In the atmosphere of a sufficiently cold brown dwarf, carbon combines with hydrogen to form **methane** molecules ($\text{CH}_4$), which strongly absorb infrared radiation at certain wavelengths. The deep methane bands are the distinctive feature of T dwarfs and make their spectrum resemble Jupiter's. In hotter objects, carbon is found mainly in carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) instead.

By comparing the new images with old photographic plates of the same region of the sky, the researchers realized that, just $32$ arcseconds away, there was a star with a high proper motion: Wolf 940. The two sources were moving against the background sky at the same speed and in the same direction. Burningham and colleagues calculated that the probability of a chance alignment was negligible: the red star and the brown dwarf travel together because they are bound by gravity. The newcomer thus became **Wolf 940B**, while the star was renamed **Wolf 940A**.

At the system's distance, $32$ arcseconds correspond to about $400$ [astronomical units]({{< relref "/dizionario/unita-astronomica/" >}}), but only as a separation projected on the plane of the sky. Allowing for the possible inclinations and eccentricities, the discoverers estimated that the brown dwarf orbits about $440$ astronomical units from the star, just under $66$ billion $\text{km}$, or $2.54$ light-days (for comparison, in November 2026, after almost $50$ years of travel, the Voyager 1 probe reached a distance of $1$ light-day from the Sun). According to their calculations, if the separation is indeed $440$ astronomical units, Wolf 940B takes about $18\,000$ years to orbit its companion. But these are estimates with a wide margin of uncertainty: the true distance most likely lies between $300$ and $800$ astronomical units and the period, accordingly, between $10\,000$ and $44\,000$ years. What we do know with reasonable confidence, from a high-resolution adaptive-optics image taken with the Keck II telescope, is that Wolf 940B is a single object, not a pair of brown dwarfs too close together to be resolved.

## How old are you, Wolf 940B?

Why all the interest in one more brown dwarf, when hundreds were already known? The answer lies in a difficulty that affects the study of all isolated brown dwarfs.

For most of its existence, a star like the Sun has a temperature and a [luminosity]({{< relref "/dizionario/luminosita/" >}}) set mainly by its mass: fusion in its core produces energy at an almost constant rate that depends on how massive the star is. A brown dwarf has no such internal energy source, apart from a brief initial phase in which it fuses deuterium, a heavy isotope of hydrogen. It is born hot, heated by the contraction of the gas cloud it formed from, and then cools continuously, growing ever fainter. *The more massive it is, the more slowly it cools*: a more massive brown dwarf is born hotter and has more heat to lose. 

The temperature and luminosity of a brown dwarf therefore depend on both mass and age. A young, low-mass brown dwarf that has not yet had time to cool can have the same temperature and luminosity as a more massive, much older one that has been cooling for a long time. From the light alone, the two cases are very hard to tell apart. To estimate the mass, temperature, or surface gravity[^gravita] of an isolated brown dwarf, astronomers must compare its spectrum with synthetic spectra from model atmospheres. But for the coldest objects those models were incomplete and, in part, still are: at the time, data on how molecules such as methane and ammonia absorb light were missing or inaccurate. Even today the models do not fully reproduce the spectra of the coldest brown dwarfs.

[^gravita]: **Surface gravity** is the gravitational acceleration at the surface of a celestial body. For a gaseous object such as a brown dwarf, which has no solid surface, it refers to the layer from which the light comes. It depends on mass and radius: for the same radius, a more massive object has a higher surface gravity. Astronomers express it as the base-$10$ logarithm of its value in centimeters per second squared, $\log g$. On Earth $g$ is about $980\ \text{cm/s}^2$, so $\log g \approx 3$; a value of $\log g = 5$ corresponds to about $100$ times Earth's gravity. Since brown dwarfs have very similar radii, surface gravity is a good indicator of their mass.

Wolf 940B offers a way around the problem, because it inherits a great deal of information from its companion. Its distance is that of Wolf 940A, whose [parallax]({{< relref "/dizionario/parallasse-trigonometrica-e-parsec/" >}}) had been known for decades. The two objects, presumably born from the same gas cloud, also share the same age and chemical composition, both of which can be estimated from the star, which is much easier to study. An object like Wolf 940B is called a ***benchmark***: a brown dwarf whose distance, age, and chemical composition are already known can be used to test whether the methods applied to all the others work. Burningham [compared](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/04/090419210430.htm) Wolf 940B to a Rosetta Stone—one that, thanks to what is known about the red dwarf it orbits, allows astronomers to decipher the message hidden in the faint light of such cold objects.

The age was deduced from the magnetic activity of Wolf 940A. Young red dwarfs are very active: they have spots, flares, and a hot chromosphere[^cromosfera], which shows up as emission in the hydrogen line known as H-alpha. Over billions of years that activity dies down. In the spectrum of Wolf 940A, H-alpha appears not in emission but only as weak absorption. 

[^cromosfera]: The **chromosphere** is the layer of a star's atmosphere that lies above the photosphere (the layer from which almost all the light comes). Within the chromosphere the temperature, instead of continuing to fall as it does through the photosphere, rises again, because the gas is heated by processes related to magnetic fields: in the Sun it goes from about $4\,000$ to about $20\,000\ \text{K}$. In red dwarfs the photosphere is too cool to produce the hydrogen H-alpha line, which forms instead in the chromosphere: in emission in very active stars, in weak absorption in less active ones. The name, from the Greek *chroma*, color, refers to the thin red rim seen around the Sun during total eclipses, whose color comes from the H-alpha line.

Burningham and colleagues drew two conclusions. First, the absence of emission indicated that the star was past its phase of strong activity, which for M4 dwarfs, according to [a study](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0004-6256/135/3/785) of tens of thousands of stars, lasts about $4.5$ billion years, with an uncertainty of $+0.5$ and $-1.0$ billion years: the system therefore had to be at least $3.5$ billion years old. The second concerned the absorption. Since a red dwarf's photosphere is too cool to produce H-alpha on its own, even in absorption, the authors took the weak absorption as a sign of residual activity. A comparison of the numbers of active and inactive M4 dwarfs suggested an age of no more than $6$ billion years. In addition, Wolf 940A's motion through the Galaxy was typical of a fairly old disk star. 

To determine the chemical composition, the researchers used a color-luminosity relation for red dwarfs that yields the **metallicity**[^metalli], obtaining a value very close to solar.

[^metalli]: In astrophysics, all chemical elements heavier than helium are called **metals**, including those that a chemist would not consider metals at all, such as carbon, oxygen, or nitrogen. **Metallicity** is the abundance of these elements in a star relative to hydrogen. It is usually expressed as $[\text{Fe/H}]$, which compares the star's iron abundance with the Sun's on a logarithmic scale: $[\text{Fe/H}] = 0$ means the same proportion as the Sun, $-1$ one tenth, $+0.3$ about twice as much. Studies also use $[\text{M/H}]$, which measures the overall abundance of all metals, not just iron, on the same logarithmic scale.

## As hot as molten lead, but cold for a brown dwarf

Knowing the distance, Burningham and colleagues were able to measure the total luminosity of Wolf 940B. They combined the spectrum they had obtained in the near-infrared, between $1$ and $2.4$ micrometers, with the [apparent magnitude]({{< relref "/dizionario/magnitudine-apparente/" >}}) measured in the *L′* band, centered on about $3.8$ micrometers. For the unobserved wavelengths they used model spectra, only to bridge the gaps. Wavelengths shorter than $1$ micrometer, which include visible light, turned out to contribute just $2\%$ of the energy emitted by the brown dwarf; $38\%$ is emitted between $1$ and $2.4$ micrometers and the remaining $60\%$ at longer wavelengths. The total luminosity was slightly less than **one millionth** of the Sun's.

Wolf 940A provided the distance, age, and chemical composition, but not the radius and mass of Wolf 940B. To derive these parameters, the researchers had to rely on evolutionary models[^modelli], which describe how brown dwarfs cool over time: given the luminosity and age, the models predict the radius, mass, and surface gravity the object must have. The radius came out at about $65\,000$ kilometers, a little over $90\%$ of Jupiter's, and the mass at $20$ to $32$ Jupiter masses. Finally, from the radius and the luminosity, the authors derived the [effective temperature]({{< relref "/dizionario/temperatura-efficace/" >}}): $570 \pm 25\ \text{K}$, or about $300$ degrees Celsius. That is roughly the melting point of lead, but for a brown dwarf it is *very low*: the most massive ones start out at about $2\,900\ \text{K}$ and, even after billions of years, stay around $2\,000\ \text{K}$.

[^modelli]: Two kinds of theoretical models are used for brown dwarfs. **Evolutionary models** describe the internal structure of the object and how it contracts and cools over time: for each mass and age they predict the radius, luminosity, and temperature. **Atmospheric models**, by contrast, compute the spectrum emitted by the outer layers for a given temperature, surface gravity, and chemical composition. The spectrum depends on the molecules present, on clouds, and on gas motions, which are difficult to model. A benchmark brown dwarf can be used to test atmospheric models, provided the radius is taken from evolutionary models.

In any case, relying on evolutionary models had little effect on the result. The effective temperature follows from the luminosity and the size of the emitting surface, that is, the radius, through the [Stefan-Boltzmann law]({{< relref "/dizionario/legge-di-stefan-boltzmann/" >}}): for the same luminosity, a smaller object must be hotter. The dependence is weak, however, because a radius $10\%$ larger lowers the temperature by only about $5\%$. Moreover, after only a few billion years, the radius of a brown dwarf changes very little over time. The authors calculated that, even for an age anywhere between $1.5$ and $10$ billion years, a far wider range than their estimate, the radius would still lie between about $58\,000$ and $73\,000$ kilometers and the temperature between $540$ and $605\ \text{K}$.

It was the first time that the properties of a brown dwarf colder than spectral type T8 had been determined without taking the temperature from a comparison with synthetic spectra computed from atmospheric models. These are precisely the models that a benchmark brown dwarf can test. When the comparison was later made, it immediately exposed their limitations. The model spectra substantially underestimated the flux in the near-infrared *K* band, centered around $2.2$ micrometers, probably because of an imperfect treatment of collision-induced absorption by molecular hydrogen. Burningham and colleagues showed that a method widely used at the time, based on flux ratios between different parts of the spectrum, would have overestimated the temperature of Wolf 940B by about $100\ \text{K}$. It was an important warning: the temperatures of the coldest T dwarfs derived with that method had to be treated with extreme caution.

A surprising feature of Wolf 940B is that, although in all likelihood it formed like a star, from the collapse of a gas cloud, it ended up resembling a planet more than a star. It is roughly the size of Jupiter and, above all, its infrared spectrum is strikingly similar to that of our gas giant. One major difference remains, however: Wolf 940B is tens of times more massive than Jupiter. This is a counterintuitive consequence of the way matter inside a brown dwarf [resists compression]({{< relref "/dizionario/pressione-di-degenerazione/" >}}): mass can increase without the radius growing.

## Spitzer's mid-infrared spectrum

As early as 2009 the discoverers announced new observations with the Spitzer Space Telescope, which operated in the infrared at wavelengths inaccessible from the ground. In 2008, through a Director's Discretionary Time program, Spitzer had imaged Wolf 940B in four bands between $3.6$ and $8$ micrometers and had obtained its spectrum between $7.5$ and $14.2$ micrometers. The spectrum alone required more than five hours of exposure, so faint was the object. The images were [analyzed](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/710/2/1627) in a study devoted to a group of cold brown dwarfs, published in 2010 and led by Sandy Leggett of the Gemini Observatory. The colors of Wolf 940B indicated a metallicity equal to or slightly higher than the Sun's, which ruled out a metal-poor system.

The spectrum was the focus of a second [study](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/720/1/252), devoted entirely to Wolf 940B, which Leggett co-authored with Burningham and with some of the leading experts in brown dwarf atmospheric models. The Spitzer spectrum alone provided almost $40\%$ of the observed light. Together with the near-infrared data, it covered just under half of the total energy emitted; the rest was filled in with models consistent with the temperature and gravity implied by the luminosity. The luminosity turned out slightly higher than the 2009 estimate: around one millionth of the Sun's.

Leggett and colleagues adopted a wider age range for the system, between $3$ and $10$ billion years. They accepted the lower limit, lowering it to $3$ billion years to be conservative, but not the residual stellar activity on which Burningham had based the upper one. The link between a red dwarf's activity and the H-alpha line, they noted, is poorly understood: weak absorption like that of Wolf 940A, typical of dwarfs of its spectral type, is also found in stars that are no longer active. The absorption, therefore, did not prove that the star was still active. Leggett and colleagues preferred not to base an upper limit on uncertain evidence and treated the star as no longer active. 

An inactive star can be any age greater than its activity lifetime: the resulting range is wider, but less likely to exclude the system's true age. They therefore derived the age range from the system's motion through the Galaxy and its metallicity, compared with those of many nearby stars of estimated age. Combining luminosity, age, and models, they concluded that Wolf 940B has an effective temperature between $585$ and $625\ \text{K}$ and a mass between $24$ and $45$ Jupiter masses. The values go together: the lowest temperature and mass correspond to the youngest age, the highest to the oldest. The spectrum alone, in any case, ruled out an age of less than one billion years.

The mid-infrared spectrum also contained information about the atmosphere. At the temperature of Wolf 940B, chemistry predicts that almost all the carbon is bound in methane and the nitrogen in ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$). Instead, the observations showed less ammonia than expected and, in the $4.5$-micrometer photometry, the signature of excess carbon monoxide, a molecule that should survive only in the deepest and hottest layers. This evidence was explained by continuous **mixing** of Wolf 940B's atmosphere: gas rises from the deep layers faster than chemical reactions can adjust its composition to the lower temperatures of the upper layers. According to the models that best reproduced the data, the mixing occurs on timescales of a few hours. The same phenomenon had been observed in other T dwarfs. On Jupiter the phenomenon had long been known: as early as 1977 Ronald Prinn and Bruce Barshay had [explained](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.198.4321.1031) the carbon monoxide in the planet's visible atmosphere as the result of rapid vertical mixing.

## Metallic uncertainties

To determine the chemical composition, Leggett and colleagues applied a revised version of the 2009 color-luminosity relation to Wolf 940A, obtaining a higher metallicity, about one and three quarters times that of the Sun: $[\text{M/H}] = +0.24 \pm 0.09\,\text{dex}$[^scalalog]. The spectrum of the brown dwarf offered an independent check. Metal-poor models fitted the spectrum better below $2$ micrometers, metal-rich ones at longer wavelengths, but neither worked across the whole spectrum. The authors concluded that the metallicity of Wolf 940B is within $0.2\,\text{dex}$ of solar, consistent with the star's.

[^scalalog]: Metallicity values are expressed on a base-$10$ logarithmic scale: they should be read as exponents of $10$, telling how many times the star's abundance of metals is greater or smaller than the Sun's, which corresponds to the value $0$. To signal this, astronomers attach the label **dex**, short for *decimal exponent*. A value of $+0.24\,\text{dex}$ corresponds to $10^{0.24} \approx 1.74$ times the solar abundance, about one and three quarters; $+0.02\,\text{dex}$ corresponds to $10^{0.02} \approx 1.05$ times, practically the same; $-0.21\,\text{dex}$ corresponds to $10^{-0.21} \approx 0.62$ times, about six tenths. A difference of $0.2\,\text{dex}$ from the solar value corresponds to a factor of $10^{0.2} \approx 1.6$, either higher or lower.

Later studies have shown that the metallicity of Wolf 940A is less certain than it might have seemed in 2010. The star was included in the sample of CARMENES, a spectrograph at the Calar Alto Observatory, in Spain, built to search for planets around red dwarfs. In 2019, from its high-resolution spectra, Schweitzer and colleagues [derived](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201834965) a metallicity practically identical to the Sun's, $[\text{Fe/H}] = +0.02 \pm 0.16\,\text{dex}$, while in 2021 a study by Marfil and colleagues, using a different analysis method, [obtained](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141980) a subsolar value: $[\text{Fe/H}] = -0.21 \pm 0.08\,\text{dex}$. Including earlier estimates, the star's metal abundance ranges from about six tenths to about one and three quarters of the Sun's, depending on the study. Since the metallicity of Wolf 940B is inferred from that of the star, the uncertainty carries over to the brown dwarf too. Leggett's conclusion, a metallicity within $0.2\,\text{dex}$ of solar, remains consistent with all these estimates within the measurement errors, but the exact value assigned to the system when Wolf 940B is used as a benchmark depends on which study one adopts.

## A demanding test for theoretical models

Within a few months, between 2009 and 2010, Wolf 940B had become the coldest brown dwarf companion to a star to have spectra and luminosity measurements in both the near- and mid-infrared. Since then many studies have used it as a point of comparison, and the picture that emerges is always the same: models struggle to reproduce the spectra of the coldest brown dwarfs, something that Wolf 940B shows particularly clearly.

In 2010 Michael Liu and colleagues, studying two pairs of T dwarfs, [concluded](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/722/1/311) that model-derived temperatures are uncertain by about $100\ \text{K}$, but no more. Among the clues pointing this way they cited the overestimate for Wolf 940B reported by its discoverers, while one of the two pairs they analyzed, ε Indi Bab, suggested if anything an underestimate of similar size. The following year a study led by Adam Burgasser [obtained](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/735/2/116) a new spectrum of Wolf 940B with the Magellan telescope, in Chile, and compared it with the models, together with the spectra of five brown dwarfs just discovered by the WISE space telescope. For Wolf 940B the best-fitting model gave a temperature of about $560\ \text{K}$, consistent with the one derived from the luminosity, but once again it could not reproduce the flux observed in the *K* band. One of the new dwarfs, **WISE J1812+2721**, had a spectrum almost identical to that of Wolf 940B; being isolated, however, it offered no way to determine its age or mass.

In 2013 Trent Dupuy and Adam Kraus uniformly [recalculated](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1241917) the properties of the coldest brown dwarfs then known, measuring many of their distances with Spitzer. For Wolf 940B, assuming an age of $5$ billion years, they obtained a temperature of $575\ \text{K}$ and a mass of about $29$ Jupiter masses. In the same year Burningham and colleagues published a [study](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stt740) of $76$ UKIDSS T dwarfs, including a list of brown dwarf companions to stars that could serve as benchmarks: Wolf 940B, which had been assigned $605\ \text{K}$, was the coldest on the list.

In 2018 Sarah Logsdon and other authors [obtained](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aade9b) a higher-resolution spectrum of Wolf 940B in the *Y* and *H* bands with the Keck telescope and compared it with four different families of models. In each family they looked for the best-matching model spectrum, but the parameters of the four best matches disagreed. The temperature, for example, ranged from $600$ to $800\ \text{K}$, whereas the one derived from the luminosity is about $570\text{-}625\ \text{K}$—another sign that the atmospheres of these objects are not yet well understood. Its unusually "blue"[^colori] *J* − *H* color was nonetheless consistent with a near-solar metallicity.

[^colori]: In astronomy, the **color** of an object is measured by comparing its brightness in two [wavelength]({{< relref "/dizionario/lunghezza-donda/" >}}) bands. An object is called "red" when it emits relatively more light in the longer-wavelength band and "blue" when it emits more in the shorter-wavelength one. In the infrared the terms are borrowed from the visible and indicate only how the light is distributed between the two bands. In the coldest T dwarfs, methane and hydrogen absorb much of the light in the *H* and *K* bands, which makes their colors very "blue."

In 2019 and 2021 the large censuses of brown dwarfs near the Sun carried out by Davy Kirkpatrick and colleagues found Wolf 940B out of place in several of their diagrams of colors and absolute magnitudes. The first [study](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/aaf6af) identified the cause: in 2008, when Spitzer imaged it at $3.6$ micrometers, the brown dwarf happened to be blended with a faint background source. Since Wolf 940B moves rapidly across the sky, the source is now well separated and visible in the more recent images from the NEOWISE mission. The discovery perhaps sheds new light on a detail of the 2010 [study](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/720/1/252). Leggett's team had noticed that no model reproduced the brightness of Wolf 940B at $3.6$ micrometers. The discrepancy had been attributed to a known flaw in the models, which in that band predicted fluxes for T dwarfs $20\text{-}50\%$ lower than those observed. It is plausible that the background source, by adding its own light to the brown dwarf's, helped widen the mismatch, but no published research has so far tested this hypothesis. The second [study](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/abd107) by Kirkpatrick's group noted that in other diagrams Wolf 940B falls among the **subdwarfs**[^subnana], but ruled this out, since its companion star is not metal-poor. It also noted that the WISE measurements reported in different catalogs disagree with one another.

[^subnana]: **Subdwarfs** are metal-poor stars or brown dwarfs that, at the same temperature, are less luminous and have different colors than objects of roughly solar composition. They are usually very old, because they formed when the gas of the Galaxy still contained few heavy elements.

The most recent study to examine it in detail, [published](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/adb39f) by Burgasser and others in 2025, proposes a system for classifying T subdwarfs according to their metallicity. Wolf 940B is one of the T dwarf companions to stars of known metallicity used to calibrate the scheme, with a new spectrum, again obtained with the Magellan telescope. From the absolute magnitudes in the *J* band and in WISE's *W2* band the authors derived a temperature of $586 \pm 35\ \text{K}$. The best-fitting atmospheric model, by contrast, gave $636\ \text{K}$ and a supersolar metallicity. The difference between the two methods, still several tens of degrees, shows that the problem raised in 2009 remains very much open. Finally, the motion of Wolf 940 through the Galaxy, recalculated with measurements from the Gaia satellite, confirms that the system is most likely part of the Galactic thin disk, the population the Sun also belongs to.

## A short-lived record

At the time of its discovery, Wolf 940B was presented as one of the coldest objects ever observed outside the Solar System. But the record did not last long. In 2010 Philip Lucas and colleagues, also from the University of Hertfordshire, [discovered](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3933.2010.00927.x) an even colder brown dwarf, **UGPS J0722−0540**, in the UKIDSS Galactic Plane Survey. It was only $4.1$ parsecs from the Sun and had a temperature of about $520\ \text{K}$. To estimate its temperature, the authors compared it with the best-studied T8.5 and T9 dwarfs, Wolf 940B among them.

In 2011 Liu and colleagues [showed](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/740/2/108) that **CFBDSIR J1458+1013** is a pair of brown dwarfs and that the fainter of the two has a temperature of just $370 \pm 40\ \text{K}$, less than $100$ degrees Celsius. To correct for systematic errors in the models, they calibrated the model predictions against the values measured for Wolf 940B. In the same year a study led by Michael Cushing [announced](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/743/1/50) the discovery, with the WISE space telescope, of the first brown dwarfs of a new spectral class, **Y**, with temperatures between $300$ and $500\ \text{K}$. The coldest known today, **WISE 0855−0714**, has a temperature of about $255$ to $285\ \text{K}$, roughly $-18$ to $+12$ degrees Celsius, depending on the method, according to James Webb Space Telescope [observations](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ad0b72) by Kevin Luhman and colleagues—not far from the temperature at which ice melts.

Wolf 940B soon lost the record for the coldest object, but it has kept its role as a *benchmark* for calibrating theoretical models. Over the years, colder brown dwarfs orbiting stars have also been found, but Wolf 940B remains one of the best studied and a reference point for testing many of the methods applied to brown dwarfs discovered since.

## How many more are out there?

In 2009 David Pinfield, one of the discoverers of Wolf 940B, [pointed out](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/04/090419210430.htm) that red dwarfs are the most numerous stars in the Galaxy and that systems like Wolf 940 might be more common than previously thought: large sky surveys could turn up many more in the Sun's neighborhood.

A recent answer came in 2026. Souleymane Ouedraogo and other authors [searched](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20673) for substellar companions around $2\,103$ stars within $20$ parsecs of the Sun, comparing proper motions measured in WISE images taken in different years. The search is sensitive to cold brown dwarfs down to about $300\ \text{K}$ and at separations of up to $20\,000$ astronomical units from the star. Wolf 940B is among the companions recovered. Correcting for objects too faint to detect, the authors estimated that about $1\%$ of the stars near the Sun have a companion of $5$ to $80$ Jupiter masses at a separation of $1000$ to $20\,000$ astronomical units. The orbit of Wolf 940B is considerably tighter than that, but the estimate gives an idea of how rare systems like Wolf 940 are. Brown dwarf companions like Wolf 940B are few and, for that very reason, valuable.

### Sources of the parameters in the table

The coordinates, spectral type, separation, estimated orbit, proper motion of the brown dwarf, *V* and *J* magnitudes, *J* − *H* color, bolometric flux, and H-alpha line come from the study by [Burningham *et al.* (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2009.14620.x); the bolometric flux was obtained by integrating the spectrum measured between $1$ and $2.4$ micrometers and adding a theoretical [bolometric correction]({{< relref "/dizionario/correzione-bolometrica/" >}}) for the unobserved wavelengths; the star's magnitudes in the *V* and *J* bands are taken from earlier catalogs. The brown dwarf's luminosity, temperature, mass, radius, and surface gravity, together with its metallicity and the wider age range, come from the study by [Leggett *et al.* (2010b)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/720/1/252). The luminosity derived in this study is slightly higher than the one the discoverers had obtained in 2009 from the bolometric flux, $\log(L/L_\odot) = -6.07 \pm 0.04$: Leggett and colleagues noted that the new value benefited from the added Spitzer mid-infrared spectrum. In any case, the difference was not significant given the measurement errors. The magnitude at $4.5\,\mu\text{m}$ comes instead from the study by [Leggett *et al.* (2010a)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/710/2/1627). 

The parallax is that of Wolf 940A measured by the Gaia satellite and reported, together with the designation GJ 1263B, by [Kirkpatrick *et al.* (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/abd107); the star's designations GJ 1263 and LHS 3708 are reported by Leggett *et al.* (2010b). The star's proper motion and radial velocity, as well as the system's membership in the thin disk, come from the study by [Burgasser *et al.* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/adb39f). The temperature, luminosity, radius, mass, and surface gravity of the star are taken from the study by [Schweitzer *et al.* (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201834965), based on spectra obtained with the CARMENES spectrograph at the Calar Alto Observatory and on the Gaia parallax: the radius is derived from the temperature and luminosity with the [Stefan-Boltzmann law]({{< relref "/dizionario/legge-di-stefan-boltzmann/" >}}), the mass from a mass-radius relation calibrated on eclipsing binaries, and the surface gravity from the mass and radius. The authors give the luminosity as a fraction of the Sun's, $0.00920 \pm 0.00011\ L_\odot$: in the table it has been converted to logarithmic form for comparison with that of the brown dwarf, which is given in the same format. 

As for the temperature of Wolf 940A, two studies give similar values: $3\,408 \pm 29\ \text{K}$ according to [Marfil *et al.* (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141980), derived from the spectrum, and $3\,294 \pm 42\ \text{K}$ according to [Houdebine *et al.* (2019)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ab23fe), derived from the star's color and from earlier literature. The metallicity of Wolf 940A, on the other hand, is rather uncertain, as the spread among published measurements shows: Burningham *et al.* (2009) obtained $[\text{Fe/H}] = -0.06 \pm 0.20\,\text{dex}$, Leggett *et al.* (2010b) $[\text{M/H}] = +0.24 \pm 0.09\,\text{dex}$, Schweitzer *et al.* (2019) $[\text{Fe/H}] = +0.02 \pm 0.16\,\text{dex}$, Houdebine *et al.* (2019) $[\text{M/H}] = +0.22\,\text{dex}$, and Marfil *et al.* (2021) $[\text{Fe/H}] = -0.21 \pm 0.08\,\text{dex}$.

The mean density is not measured, but is calculated by dividing the mass by the volume of a sphere of the same radius. For Wolf 940A, with a mass of $0.276$ solar masses ($5.49 \times 10^{32}\,\text{g}$) and a radius of $0.284$ solar radii ($1.98 \times 10^{10}\,\text{cm}$) we obtain:

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{5.49 \times 10^{32}\ \text{g}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi\,(1.98 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm})^3} \approx \frac{5.49 \times 10^{32}\ \text{g}}{3.23 \times 10^{31}\ \text{cm}^3} \approx 17\ \text{g/cm}^3$$

that is, about $12$ times the mean density of the Sun. The same calculation for Wolf 940B gives values from $35\ \text{g/cm}^3$, for the mass and radius corresponding to the youngest age ($24$ Jupiter masses and $0.097$ solar radii), to $100\ \text{g/cm}^3$ for those of the oldest age ($45$ Jupiter masses and $0.084$ solar radii). The brown dwarf is six to twelve times less massive than the star, but two to six times denser—an effect of degenerate matter, which in brown dwarfs is compressed more strongly the greater the mass. The surface gravity of the two objects, on the other hand, is almost the same, because the star's greater mass is offset by its radius, about three times larger.

In the table rows from temperature to density, the lowest values correspond to the youngest age and the highest to the oldest. Later studies have given temperatures between about $560$ and $640\ \text{K}$, depending on the method: the most recent luminosity-based estimate is $586 \pm 35\ \text{K}$ (Burgasser *et al.* 2025).
