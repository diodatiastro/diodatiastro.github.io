{{% box tipo="definizione" titolo="Wolf 940B at a glance" %}} 
Wolf 940B is a **brown dwarf**[^nanabruna] orbiting a small red star, Wolf 940A, a little over $40$ light-years from the Sun. It was discovered in 2008 and described in a study published in early 2009. It is slightly smaller than Jupiter, but between $20$ and $45$ times more massive, depending on the estimated age. With a temperature of about $600\ \text{K}$, just over $300$ degrees Celsius, it was at the time one of the coldest objects ever observed outside the Solar System, a record it lost in the following years, after the discovery of even colder brown dwarfs.  Its importance, however, lies not only in its low temperature, but in the fact that it is not an isolated object: Wolf 940B was the first brown dwarf this cold whose distance, age and chemical composition could be known with confidence, thanks to the star it orbits. It thus became a reference object, a sort of standard yardstick against which astronomers test the theoretical models of the coldest atmospheres: those of brown dwarfs and, one day, of the giant planets discovered around other stars.

[^nanabruna]: A **brown dwarf** is an object that forms like a star, from the collapse of a gas cloud, but with a mass too small to ignite stable hydrogen-to-helium fusion in its core, the process that makes main-sequence stars like the Sun shine. For a chemical composition similar to the Sun's, the limit below which fusion does not ignite is around $0.072$ solar masses, that is, about $75$ times the mass of Jupiter. Deprived of the energy source that powers stars, a brown dwarf cools and fades slowly and indefinitely. The discovery of the first brown dwarf was confirmed in 1995.

## A brown companion for a red dwarf

In the constellation of Aquarius, about $12.4$ parsecs[^parsec] from the Sun, lies a small **red dwarf**[^nanarossa] of class **M4**[^classi], too faint to be seen with the naked eye. It was first reported in 1919 by the German astronomer Max Wolf, who at Heidelberg was combing through photographic plates of the sky in search of stars with a high proper motion[^motoproprio], a sign of their closeness to the Sun. For this star he [measured](https://scixplorer.org/abs/1919VeHei...7..195W) a displacement of $0.93''$ per year, very close to today's value. The star has borne his name ever since: **Wolf 940**. It has about a quarter of the Sun's mass and, like the vast majority of stars in the Galaxy, there is nothing special about it.

[^nanarossa]: **Red dwarfs** are the smallest and coolest stars of the main sequence (the long phase in which stars produce energy by fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores). They belong to spectral class M, have masses between about $0.08$ and $0.6$ solar masses and effective temperatures between about $2\,300$ and $3\,900\ \text{K}$. They are faint, from a few hundredths to less than a thousandth of the Sun's luminosity, which is why hardly any of them is visible to the naked eye. They are, however, the most numerous stars: counting the stars and brown dwarfs within $10$ parsecs of the Sun, Reylé and colleagues [found](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202140985), by analyzing data from the Gaia astrometric satellite, that about $61\%$ are red dwarfs, more than half of which have a spectral type between M3 and M5, like Wolf 940. They burn their nuclear fuel so slowly that they remain on the main sequence far longer than the present age of the universe: according to the models of [Laughlin, Bodenheimer and Adams](https://doi.org/10.1086/304125), about one and a half trillion years for a red dwarf of $0.2$ solar masses, and as much as ten trillion years for the smallest ones, those close to the limit of $0.08$ solar masses.

[^parsec]: The [parsec]({{< relref "/dizionario/parallasse-trigonometrica-e-parsec/" >}}#calcolare-le-distanze-stellari-in-parsec) is one of the units of distance used in astronomy and corresponds to about $3.26$ light-years. $12.4$ parsecs is therefore a little over $40$ light-years.

[^classi]: Stars and brown dwarfs are arranged in **spectral classes** according to their effective temperature, which can be recognized from the lines and bands present in the [spectrum]({{< relref "/dizionario/spettro-elettromagnetico/" >}}). After the hottest classes (O, B, A, F, G, K) come the red dwarfs of class M and then classes L, T and Y, introduced from the late 1990s onward for ever colder objects. Classes T and Y consist only of brown dwarfs. A number from $0$ to $9$, placed after the letter, expresses a temperature scale within the class: $0$ indicates the highest temperature, $9$ the lowest. Half types, such as T8.5, are also used.

[^motoproprio]: **Proper motion** is the apparent displacement of a star on the celestial sphere, due to its real motion through space relative to the Sun. It is measured in arcseconds (or thousandths of an arcsecond) per year and indicates the transverse displacement of the object across the sky, perpendicular to our line of sight. For the vast majority of stars it is tiny; a large displacement usually indicates a nearby object.

Almost ninety years later, Wolf 940 turned out to have a much smaller and colder companion. The discovery came from **UKIDSS** (*UKIRT Infrared Deep Sky Survey*), a large mapping of the sky in the infrared[^infrarosso] carried out with the *United Kingdom Infrared Telescope* (UKIRT), a $3.8$-meter telescope on the summit of Mauna Kea, in Hawaii. One of the goals of the survey was to find the coldest brown dwarfs in the Sun's neighborhood: objects that can hardly be observed in visible light, but that shine faintly in the infrared.

[^infrarosso]: **Infrared** radiation has wavelengths longer than those of red light and is invisible to the human eye. Relatively cold objects emit most of their energy precisely in the infrared. Astronomers divide the near-infrared into bands designated by letters: the most widely used are *Y*, *J*, *H* and *K*, centered respectively on about $1.0$, $1.25$, $1.65$ and $2.2$ micrometers.

In a UKIDSS image taken in October 2007, a team led by Ben Burningham, of the University of Hertfordshire, noticed an extremely faint source with the infrared colors typical of the coldest T-class brown dwarfs. During 2008 the team observed it with a series of telescopes in Chile, the Canary Islands and Hawaii, and in early 2009 [announced](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2009.14620.x) the discovery. The spectrum obtained with the Gemini North telescope, on Mauna Kea, showed the deep water and methane[^metano] absorption bands that characterize T dwarfs, and made it possible to classify the object as **T8.5**: among the coldest brown dwarfs known at the time.

[^metano]: In the atmosphere of a sufficiently cold brown dwarf, carbon combines with hydrogen to form **methane** molecules ($\text{CH}_4$), which strongly absorb infrared radiation at certain wavelengths. The deep methane bands are the distinctive feature of class T dwarfs and make their spectrum resemble Jupiter's. In hotter objects, carbon is found mainly in carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) instead.

By comparing the new images with those on old photographic plates of the same region of the sky, the researchers realized that, just $32$ arcseconds away, there was a star with a high proper motion: Wolf 940. The two sources were moving against the background sky at the same speed and in the same direction. Burningham and colleagues calculated that the probability of a chance alignment was negligible: the red star and the brown dwarf travel together because they are bound by gravity. The newcomer thus became **Wolf 940B**, while the star was renamed **Wolf 940A**.

At the distance of the system, $32$ arcseconds correspond to a separation of about $400$ [astronomical units]({{< relref "/dizionario/unita-astronomica/" >}}), but only as projected on the plane of the sky. Taking into account the possible inclinations and eccentricities, the discoverers estimated that the brown dwarf orbits about $440$ astronomical units from the star, that is, just under $66$ billion $\text{km}$, equal to $2.54$ light-days (for comparison, in November 2026 the Voyager 1 probe reached, after almost $50$ years of travel, a distance of $1$ light-day from the Sun). According to the calculations, if the separation from the red dwarf is indeed $440$ astronomical units, Wolf 940B needs about $18\,000$ years to complete one orbit around its companion. But these are estimates with a wide margin of uncertainty: the true distance most likely lies between $300$ and $800$ astronomical units and the period, accordingly, between $10\,000$ and $44\,000$ years. What we know with relative certainty, thanks to a high-resolution image obtained with the adaptive optics of the Keck II telescope, is that Wolf 940B is a single object and not a pair of brown dwarfs too close together to be resolved.

## How old are you, Wolf 940B?

Why so much interest in one more brown dwarf, when hundreds were already known? The answer lies in a difficulty that affects the study of all isolated brown dwarfs.

For most of its existence, a star like the Sun has a temperature and a [luminosity]({{< relref "/dizionario/luminosita/" >}}) set mainly by its mass: the fusion reactions in the core produce energy at an almost constant rate, which depends on how massive the star is. A brown dwarf has no such internal energy source, apart from a brief initial phase in which it fuses deuterium, a heavy isotope of hydrogen. It is born hot, from the heat released by the contraction of the gas cloud from which it formed, and then cools continuously, becoming ever fainter. *The more massive it is, the more slowly it cools*: a more massive brown dwarf is born hotter and has more heat to lose. 

The temperature and luminosity of a brown dwarf therefore depend on both mass and age. A light but young brown dwarf, which has not yet had time to cool, can have the same temperature and luminosity as a more massive but much older one that has been cooling for a long time. From its light alone, it is very difficult to tell the two possibilities apart. To estimate the mass, temperature or surface gravity[^gravita] of an isolated brown dwarf, astronomers have to compare its spectrum with those calculated from theoretical models of atmospheres. But for the coldest objects those models were incomplete and, in part, still are: at the time, data on the absorption of light by molecules such as methane and ammonia were missing or inaccurate. Even today the models do not fully reproduce the spectra of the coldest brown dwarfs.

[^gravita]: **Surface gravity** is the gravitational acceleration at the surface of a celestial body. For a gaseous object such as a brown dwarf, which has no solid surface, it refers to the layer from which the light comes. It depends on mass and radius: for the same radius, a more massive object has a higher surface gravity. Astronomers express it as the base-$10$ logarithm of its value in centimeters per second squared, $\log g$. On Earth $g$ is about $980\ \text{cm/s}^2$, that is, $\log g \approx 3$; a value of $\log g = 5$ corresponds to a gravity about $100$ times that of Earth. Since brown dwarfs have very similar radii, surface gravity is a good indicator of their mass.

Wolf 940B makes it possible to get around the difficulty, because it inherits a great deal of information from its companion. Its distance is that of Wolf 940A, whose [parallax]({{< relref "/dizionario/parallasse-trigonometrica-e-parsec/" >}}) had been measured for decades. The two objects, presumably born from the same gas cloud, also share the same age and the same chemical composition, which can be estimated from the star, much easier to study. An object with the characteristics of Wolf 940B is called a ***benchmark***: a brown dwarf whose distance, age and chemical composition are already known can be used, in fact, to check whether the methods applied to all the others work. Burningham [compared](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/04/090419210430.htm) Wolf 940B to a Rosetta Stone which, thanks to what is known about the red dwarf it orbits, makes it possible to decipher the message contained in the faint light of such cold objects.

The age was deduced from the magnetic activity of Wolf 940A. Young red dwarfs are very active: they have spots, flares and a hot chromosphere[^cromosfera] that can be recognized by the hydrogen line called H-alpha, present in emission in their spectrum. Over billions of years that activity dies down. In the spectrum of Wolf 940A the H-alpha line does not appear in emission, but only as weak absorption. 

[^cromosfera]: The **chromosphere** is the layer of a star's atmosphere that lies above the photosphere (the layer from which almost all the light comes). Within the chromosphere the temperature, instead of continuing to fall as it does through the photosphere, rises again, because the gas is heated by processes related to magnetic fields: in the Sun it goes from about $4\,000$ to about $20\,000\ \text{K}$. In red dwarfs the photosphere is too cool to produce the hydrogen H-alpha line, which forms instead in the chromosphere: in emission in very active stars, in weak absorption in less active ones. The name, from the Greek *chroma*, color, recalls the thin red rim seen around the Sun during total eclipses, which is due precisely to the H-alpha line.

Burningham and colleagues drew two conclusions. The first was that the absence of emission indicated that the star had passed its phase of strong activity, which for M4-type dwarfs, according to [a study](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/0004-6256/135/3/785) of tens of thousands of stars, lasts about $4.5$ billion years, with an uncertainty of $+0.5$ and $-1.0$ billion years: the system therefore had to be at least $3.5$ billion years old. The second concerned the absorption. Since the photosphere of a red dwarf is too cool to produce the H-alpha line on its own, even in absorption, according to the authors the weak absorption observed revealed some residual activity. Comparing the numbers of active and inactive M4 dwarfs suggested an age of no more than $6$ billion years. In addition, the motion of Wolf 940A through the Galaxy turned out to be the one typical of a fairly old disk star. 

For the chemical composition, the researchers used a relation between the color and luminosity of red dwarfs that yields the **metallicity**[^metalli], and obtained a value very close to the solar one.

[^metalli]: In astrophysics, all chemical elements heavier than helium are called **metals**, including those that a chemist would not consider metals at all, such as carbon, oxygen or nitrogen. **Metallicity** is the abundance of these elements in a star relative to hydrogen. It is usually measured with the index $[\text{Fe/H}]$, which compares the abundance of iron with that of the Sun on a logarithmic scale: $[\text{Fe/H}] = 0$ means the same proportion as the Sun, $-1$ one tenth, $+0.3$ about twice as much. Studies also use the index $[\text{M/H}]$, which measures on the same logarithmic scale the overall abundance of all metals and not just that of iron.

## As hot as molten lead, but cold for a brown dwarf

Knowing the distance, Burningham and colleagues were able to measure the total luminosity of Wolf 940B. They combined the spectrum they had obtained in the near-infrared, between $1$ and $2.4$ micrometers, with the [apparent magnitude]({{< relref "/dizionario/magnitudine-apparente/" >}}) measured in the *L′* band, centered on about $3.8$ micrometers. For the wavelengths not observed they used theoretical spectra, only to bridge the gaps. It turned out that wavelengths shorter than $1$ micrometer, which include visible light, contribute just $2\%$ of the energy emitted by the brown dwarf; $38\%$ comes out between $1$ and $2.4$ micrometers and the remaining $60\%$ at longer wavelengths. The total luminosity was slightly less than **one millionth** of the Sun's.

Wolf 940A provided the distance, age and chemical composition, but not the radius and mass of Wolf 940B. To derive these parameters, the researchers had to rely on evolutionary models[^modelli], which describe how brown dwarfs cool over time: given the luminosity and age, the models indicate the radius, mass and surface gravity the object must have. The radius came out at about $65\,000$ kilometers, a little over $90\%$ of Jupiter's, and the mass between $20$ and $32$ times that of Jupiter. Finally, from the radius and the luminosity, the authors derived the [effective temperature]({{< relref "/dizionario/temperatura-efficace/" >}}): $570 \pm 25\ \text{K}$, that is, about $300$ degrees Celsius. That is roughly the temperature at which lead melts, but for a brown dwarf it is *very low*: when newly formed, the most massive ones reach about $2\,900\ \text{K}$ and, even after billions of years, they remain around $2\,000\ \text{K}$.

[^modelli]: Two kinds of theoretical models are used for brown dwarfs. **Evolutionary models** describe the internal structure of the object and how it contracts and cools over time: for each mass and age they predict the radius, luminosity and temperature. **Atmospheric models**, on the other hand, calculate the spectrum emitted by the outermost layers for a given temperature, surface gravity and chemical composition. The spectrum depends on the molecules present, on clouds and on gas motions, phenomena that are difficult to describe. A benchmark brown dwarf makes it possible to test atmospheric models, provided one relies on evolutionary models for the radius.

In any case, relying on evolutionary models had little effect on the result. The effective temperature is derived from the luminosity and from the extent of the surface that emits it, that is, from the radius, according to the [Stefan-Boltzmann law]({{< relref "/dizionario/legge-di-stefan-boltzmann/" >}}): for the same luminosity, a smaller object must be hotter. The dependence is weak, however, because a radius $10\%$ larger lowers the temperature by only about $5\%$. Moreover, after only a few billion years, the radius of a brown dwarf changes very little over time. The authors calculated that, even with an age between $1.5$ and $10$ billion years, far more uncertain than the estimated one, the radius would have been between about $58\,000$ and $73\,000$ kilometers and the temperature between $540$ and $605\ \text{K}$.

It was the first time that the properties of a brown dwarf colder than class T8 had been determined without deriving the temperature from a comparison with theoretical spectra, calculated with atmospheric models. These are precisely the models that a benchmark brown dwarf makes it possible to test. The comparison, made afterwards, immediately revealed their limitations. The calculated spectra considerably underestimated the light emitted in the near-infrared *K* band, centered around $2.2$ micrometers, probably because they treated imperfectly the collision-induced absorption by hydrogen molecules. Burningham and colleagues showed that a method widely used at the time, based on the ratios between the fluxes measured in different parts of the spectrum, would have overestimated the temperature of Wolf 940B by about $100\ \text{K}$. It was an important warning: the temperatures of the coldest T dwarfs derived with that method had to be treated with extreme caution.

A surprising feature of Wolf 940B is that, although in all likelihood it formed like a star, from the collapse of a gas cloud, it ended up becoming something that resembles a planet more than a star. It is, in fact, roughly the size of Jupiter, but above all, when its infrared spectrum is compared with that of our gas giant, a striking similarity emerges. A major difference nonetheless remains between the two bodies: Wolf 940B is tens of times more massive than Jupiter. This is a counterintuitive consequence of the way the matter of a brown dwarf [resists compression]({{< relref "/dizionario/pressione-di-degenerazione/" >}}): the mass can grow without the radius increasing.

## Spitzer's mid-infrared spectrum

As early as 2009 the discoverers announced new observations with the Spitzer Space Telescope, which worked in the infrared at wavelengths inaccessible from the ground. In 2008, through a Director's Discretionary Time program, Spitzer had imaged Wolf 940B in four bands between $3.6$ and $8$ micrometers and had obtained its spectrum between $7.5$ and $14.2$ micrometers. The spectrum alone required more than five hours of exposure, so faint was the object. The images were [analyzed](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/710/2/1627) in a study devoted to a group of cold brown dwarfs, published in 2010 and led by Sandy Leggett of the Gemini Observatory. The colors of Wolf 940B indicated a metallicity equal to or slightly higher than the Sun's, which ruled out a metal-poor system.

The spectrum was at the center of a second [study](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/720/1/252), devoted entirely to Wolf 940B, which Leggett co-authored with Burningham and with some of the leading experts in brown dwarf atmospheric models. The Spitzer spectrum alone provided almost $40\%$ of the observed light. Together with the near-infrared data, it covered just under half of the total energy emitted; for the rest, models were used, choosing those consistent with the temperature and gravity implied by the luminosity. The luminosity turned out slightly higher than the 2009 estimate: around one millionth of the Sun's.

Leggett and colleagues adopted a wider age range for the system, between $3$ and $10$ billion years. They accepted the lower limit, lowering it to $3$ billion years as a precaution, but not the residual stellar activity on which Burningham had based the upper one. The link between a red dwarf's activity and the H-alpha line, they noted, is poorly understood: weak absorption like that of Wolf 940A, typical of dwarfs of its class, is also found in stars that are no longer active. The absorption, therefore, did not prove that the star was still active. Leggett and colleagues preferred not to base an upper limit on uncertain evidence and treated the star as no longer active. 

An inactive star can have any age greater than the duration of the active phase: the resulting range is wider, but it runs less risk of excluding the true age of the system. They therefore derived the range from the system's motion through the Galaxy and from its metallicity, compared with those of many stars near the Sun whose ages had been estimated. Combining luminosity, age and models, they concluded that Wolf 940B has an effective temperature between $585$ and $625\ \text{K}$ and a mass between $24$ and $45$ times that of Jupiter. The values are linked to each other: the lowest temperature and mass correspond to the lowest age estimate, the highest ones to the highest estimate. The spectrum alone, in any case, ruled out an age of less than one billion years.

The mid-infrared spectrum also contained information about the atmosphere. At the temperature of Wolf 940B, chemistry predicts that almost all the carbon is bound in methane and the nitrogen in ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$). The observations showed instead less ammonia than expected and, in the $4.5$-micrometer measurement, the signature of an excess of carbon monoxide, a molecule that should survive only in the deepest and hottest layers. This spectral evidence was explained by the continuous **mixing** of the atmosphere of Wolf 940B: gas rises from the deep layers faster than chemical reactions can adjust its composition to the lower temperatures of the upper layers. According to the models that best reproduced the data, the mixing takes place on timescales of the order of a few hours. The same phenomenon had been observed in other T-class brown dwarfs. On Jupiter it had been known for a long time: as early as 1977 Ronald Prinn and Bruce Barshay had [explained](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.198.4321.1031) the presence of carbon monoxide in the visible layer of the planet's atmosphere by rapid vertical mixing.

## Metallic uncertainties

For the chemical composition, Leggett and colleagues applied to Wolf 940A a revised version of the color-luminosity relation used in 2009 and obtained a higher metallicity, about one and three quarters times that of the Sun: $[\text{M/H}] = +0.24 \pm 0.09\,\text{dex}$[^scalalog]. The spectrum of the brown dwarf offered an independent check. The more metal-poor models fitted the part of the spectrum below $2$ micrometers better, the more metal-rich ones the part at longer wavelengths, but neither worked across the whole spectrum. The authors concluded that the metallicity of Wolf 940B does not differ from the solar value by more than $0.2$ dex, in agreement with the star's value.

[^scalalog]: Metallicity values are expressed on a base-$10$ logarithmic scale: they should be read as exponents of $10$, telling how many times the star's abundance of metals is greater or smaller than the Sun's, which corresponds to the value $0$. As a reminder, astronomers attach to them the abbreviation **dex**, short for *decimal exponent*. A value of $+0.24\,\text{dex}$ corresponds to $10^{0.24} \approx 1.74$ times the solar abundance, about one and three quarters; $+0.02\,\text{dex}$ corresponds to $10^{0.02} \approx 1.05$ times, that is, almost the same; $-0.21\,\text{dex}$ corresponds to $10^{-0.21} \approx 0.62$ times, about six tenths. A difference of $0.2\,\text{dex}$ from the solar value corresponds to a factor of $10^{0.2} \approx 1.6$, either higher or lower.

Later studies have shown that the metallicity of Wolf 940A is less certain than it might have seemed in 2010. The star was included in the sample of CARMENES, a spectrograph at the Calar Alto Observatory, in Spain, built to search for planets around red dwarfs. From its high-resolution spectra Schweitzer and colleagues [derived](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201834965) in 2019 a metallicity practically equal to the solar one, $[\text{Fe/H}] = +0.02 \pm 0.16\,\text{dex}$, while a study by Marfil and other authors, using a different analysis method, [obtained](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141980) in 2021 a value lower than the solar one: $[\text{Fe/H}] = -0.21 \pm 0.08\,\text{dex}$. Taking earlier estimates into account as well, the star's abundance of metals ranges from about six tenths to about one and three quarters that of the Sun, depending on the study. Since the metallicity of Wolf 940B is inferred from that of the star, the uncertainty carries over to the brown dwarf too. The conclusion of Leggett's study, namely a metallicity that does not differ from the solar value by more than $0.2\,\text{dex}$, remains compatible, within the measurement errors, with all these estimates, but the precise value attributed to the system when Wolf 940B is used as a benchmark depends on which study one chooses.

## A demanding test for theoretical models

Within a few months, between 2009 and 2010, Wolf 940B had become the coldest of the brown dwarf companions of stars for which spectra and luminosity measurements existed in both the near- and the mid-infrared. Since then many studies have used it as a point of comparison, and the picture that emerges is always the same: models struggle to reproduce the spectra of the coldest brown dwarfs, something that Wolf 940B shows particularly clearly.

In 2010 Michael Liu and colleagues, studying two pairs of T-class brown dwarfs, [concluded](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/722/1/311) that the temperatures derived from models are affected by errors of about $100\ \text{K}$, but not larger. Among the clues pointing this way they cited the overestimate for Wolf 940B reported by its discoverers, while one of the two pairs they analyzed, ε Indi Bab, suggested if anything an underestimate of similar size. The following year a study led by Adam Burgasser [obtained](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/735/2/116) a new spectrum of Wolf 940B with the Magellan telescope, in Chile, and compared it with the models, together with the spectra of five brown dwarfs just discovered by the WISE space telescope. For Wolf 940B the best model gave a temperature of about $560\ \text{K}$, compatible with the one derived from the luminosity, but once again it could not reproduce the flux observed in the *K* band. One of the new dwarfs, **WISE J1812+2721**, had a spectrum almost identical to that of Wolf 940B: however, since it was isolated, no information could be derived about either its age or its mass.

In 2013 Trent Dupuy and Adam Kraus [recalculated](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1241917) in a uniform way the properties of the coldest brown dwarfs then known, measuring many of their distances with Spitzer. For Wolf 940B, assuming an age of $5$ billion years, they obtained a temperature of $575\ \text{K}$ and a mass of about $29$ times that of Jupiter. In the same year Burningham and colleagues published a [study](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stt740) of $76$ UKIDSS T dwarfs, with a list of brown dwarf companions of stars that could be used as benchmarks: Wolf 940B, which had been assigned $605\ \text{K}$, appeared in it as the coldest.

In 2018 Sarah Logsdon and other authors [obtained](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aade9b) with the Keck telescope a higher-resolution spectrum of Wolf 940B, in the *Y* and *H* bands, and compared it with four different families of models. In each family they looked for the theoretical spectrum most similar to the observed one, but the parameters of the four spectra found in this way did not agree with one another. The temperature, for example, ranged from $600$ to $800\ \text{K}$, whereas the one derived from the luminosity is about $570$-$625\ \text{K}$: another sign that the atmospheres of these objects are not yet well understood. Its unusually "blue"[^colori] *J* − *H* color was nonetheless consistent with a metallicity close to the solar one.

[^colori]: In astronomy, the **color** of an object is measured by comparing its brightness in two [wavelength]({{< relref "/dizionario/lunghezza-donda/" >}}) bands. An object is called "red" when it emits relatively more light in the longer-wavelength band and "blue" when it emits more in the shorter-wavelength one. In the infrared the terms are borrowed from the visible and indicate only how the light is distributed between the two bands. In the coldest T dwarfs, methane and hydrogen absorb much of the light in the *H* and *K* bands, which makes their colors very "blue".

In 2019 and 2021 the large censuses of brown dwarfs near the Sun carried out by Davy Kirkpatrick and colleagues found Wolf 940B out of place in some diagrams that relate colors and luminosities. The first [study](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/aaf6af) identified the cause: in 2008, when Spitzer imaged it at $3.6$ micrometers, the brown dwarf happened to overlap a faint background source. Since Wolf 940B moves rapidly across the sky, the source is now well separated and visible in the more recent images from the NEOWISE mission. The discovery perhaps sheds new light on a detail of the 2010 [study](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/720/1/252). Leggett's team had noticed that no model reproduced the brightness of Wolf 940B at $3.6$ micrometers. The discrepancy had been attributed to a known flaw of the models, which in that band predicted fluxes for T dwarfs $20$-$50\%$ lower than those observed. It is plausible that the background source, adding its own light to that of the brown dwarf, contributed to increasing the mismatch, but no published research has so far tested this hypothesis. The second [study](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/abd107) by Kirkpatrick's group noted that in other diagrams Wolf 940B falls among the **subdwarfs**[^subnana], but ruled out that it is one, since the metallicity of its companion star is not low. It also noted that the WISE measurements reported in different catalogs disagree with one another.

[^subnana]: **Subdwarfs** are metal-poor stars or brown dwarfs that, at the same temperature, are less luminous and have different colors compared with objects of a composition similar to the Sun's. They are usually very old, because they formed when the gas of the Galaxy still contained few heavy elements.

The most recent study to examine it in detail, [published](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/adb39f) by Burgasser and others in 2025, proposes a system for classifying T-class subdwarfs according to their metallicity. Wolf 940B appears in it among the T dwarf companions of stars with known metallicity, used to calibrate the system, with a new spectrum obtained once again with the Magellan telescope. From the absolute magnitudes in the *J* band and in WISE's *W2* band the authors derived a temperature of $586 \pm 35\ \text{K}$. The atmospheric model that best fitted the spectrum gave instead $636\ \text{K}$ and a higher-than-solar metallicity. The difference between the two methods, still several tens of degrees, shows how much the problem raised in 2009 remains open. Finally, the motion of Wolf 940 through the Galaxy, recalculated with measurements from the Gaia satellite, confirms that the system most likely belongs to the thin disk of the Galaxy, the population to which the Sun also belongs.

## A short-lived record

At the time of its discovery, Wolf 940B was presented as one of the coldest objects ever observed outside the Solar System. But the record did not last long. In 2010 Philip Lucas and colleagues, also from the University of Hertfordshire, [discovered](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3933.2010.00927.x) in the part of UKIDSS devoted to the plane of the Galaxy an even colder brown dwarf, **UGPS J0722−0540**. It was only $4.1$ parsecs from the Sun and had a temperature of about $520\ \text{K}$. To estimate it, the authors compared it precisely with the best-studied T8.5 and T9 dwarfs, including Wolf 940B.

In 2011 Liu and colleagues [showed](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/740/2/108) that **CFBDSIR J1458+1013** is a pair of brown dwarfs and that the fainter of the two has a temperature of just $370 \pm 40\ \text{K}$, less than $100$ degrees Celsius. To correct the systematic errors of the models, they adjusted their predictions to the values measured for Wolf 940B. In the same year a study led by Michael Cushing [announced](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/743/1/50) the discovery, thanks to the WISE space telescope, of the first brown dwarfs of a new spectral class, **Y**, with temperatures between $300$ and $500\ \text{K}$. The coldest known today, **WISE 0855−0714**, has a temperature between about $255$ and $285\ \text{K}$, that is, between about $-18$ and $+12$ degrees Celsius, depending on the method used to estimate it, according to the [observations](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ad0b72) made with the James Webb Space Telescope by Kevin Luhman and colleagues: not far from the temperature at which ice melts.

Wolf 940B soon lost the record for the coldest object, but it has kept its role as a *benchmark* for calibrating theoretical models. Over the years colder brown dwarfs have been found, also companions of stars, but Wolf 940B remains one of the best studied and a reference point for testing many of the methods applied to the brown dwarfs discovered later.

## How many more are out there?

In 2009 David Pinfield, one of the discoverers of Wolf 940B, [observed](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/04/090419210430.htm) that red dwarfs are the most numerous stars in the Galaxy and that systems similar to Wolf 940 might be more common than had been thought: the large sky surveys could find many more of them in the Sun's neighborhood.

A recent answer came in 2026. Souleymane Ouedraogo and other authors [searched](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.20673) for substellar companions around $2\,103$ stars within $20$ parsecs of the Sun, comparing the proper motions measured in WISE images taken in different years. The search is sensitive to cold brown dwarfs down to about $300\ \text{K}$ and at distances from the star of up to $20\,000$ astronomical units. Wolf 940B is among the companions recovered. Taking into account objects too faint to be seen, the authors estimated that about $1\%$ of the stars near the Sun have a companion with a mass between $5$ and $80$ times that of Jupiter, at a distance between $1000$ and $20\,000$ astronomical units. The orbit of Wolf 940B is considerably tighter than that, but the estimate gives an idea of how rare systems like Wolf 940 are. Companions of stars like Wolf 940B are few and, for that very reason, precious.

### Sources of the parameters in the table

The coordinates, spectral class, separation, estimated orbit, proper motion of the brown dwarf, *V* and *J* magnitudes, *J* − *H* color, bolometric flux and H-alpha line come from the study by [Burningham *et al.* (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2966.2009.14620.x); the bolometric flux was obtained by integrating the spectrum measured between $1$ and $2.4$ micrometers and adding a theoretical [bolometric correction]({{< relref "/dizionario/correzione-bolometrica/" >}}) for the wavelengths not observed; the star's magnitudes in the *V* and *J* bands are taken from earlier catalogs. The luminosity, temperature, mass, radius and surface gravity of the brown dwarf, its metallicity and the wider age range come from the study by [Leggett *et al.* (2010b)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/720/1/252). The luminosity derived in this study is slightly higher than the one the discoverers had obtained in 2009 from the bolometric flux, $\log(L/L_\odot) = -6.07 \pm 0.04$: Leggett and colleagues specified that the new value benefited from the addition of the Spitzer mid-infrared spectrum. In any case, the difference was not significant compared with the measurement errors. The magnitude at $4.5\,\mu\text{m}$ comes instead from the study by [Leggett *et al.* (2010a)](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/710/2/1627). 

The parallax is that of Wolf 940A measured by the Gaia satellite and reported, together with the designation GJ 1263B, by [Kirkpatrick *et al.* (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4365/abd107); the star's designations GJ 1263 and LHS 3708 are reported by Leggett *et al.* (2010b). The star's proper motion and radial velocity, as well as the system's membership in the thin disk, come from the study by [Burgasser *et al.* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/adb39f). The temperature, luminosity, radius, mass and surface gravity of the star are taken from the study by [Schweitzer *et al.* (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201834965), based on spectra obtained with the CARMENES spectrograph at the Calar Alto Observatory and on the Gaia parallax: the radius is derived from the temperature and luminosity with the [Stefan-Boltzmann law]({{< relref "/dizionario/legge-di-stefan-boltzmann/" >}}), the mass from a mass-radius relation calibrated on eclipsing binary stars, and the surface gravity from the mass and radius. The authors give the luminosity as a fraction of the Sun's, $0.00920 \pm 0.00011\ L_\odot$: in the table it has been converted to logarithmic form for comparison with that of the brown dwarf, which is given in the same format. 

As for the temperature of Wolf 940A, two studies give similar values: $3\,408 \pm 29\ \text{K}$ according to [Marfil *et al.* (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202141980), derived from the spectrum, and $3\,294 \pm 42\ \text{K}$ according to [Houdebine *et al.* (2019)](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/ab23fe), derived from the star's color and from earlier literature. The metallicity of Wolf 940A, on the other hand, is rather uncertain, as can be seen from the differences between the individual published measurements: Burningham *et al.* (2009) obtained $[\text{Fe/H}] = -0.06 \pm 0.20\,\text{dex}$, Leggett *et al.* (2010b) $[\text{M/H}] = +0.24 \pm 0.09\,\text{dex}$, Schweitzer *et al.* (2019) $[\text{Fe/H}] = +0.02 \pm 0.16\,\text{dex}$, Houdebine *et al.* (2019) $[\text{M/H}] = +0.22\,\text{dex}$ and Marfil *et al.* (2021) $[\text{Fe/H}] = -0.21 \pm 0.08\,\text{dex}$.

The mean density is not measured, but is calculated by dividing the mass by the volume of a sphere of the same radius. For Wolf 940A, with a mass of $0.276$ solar masses ($5.49 \times 10^{32}\,\text{g}$) and a radius of $0.284$ solar radii ($1.98 \times 10^{10}\,\text{cm}$) we obtain:

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{5.49 \times 10^{32}\ \text{g}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi\,(1.98 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm})^3} \approx \frac{5.49 \times 10^{32}\ \text{g}}{3.23 \times 10^{31}\ \text{cm}^3} \approx 17\ \text{g/cm}^3$$

that is, about $12$ times the mean density of the Sun. The same calculation for Wolf 940B gives between $35\ \text{g/cm}^3$, for the mass and radius corresponding to the youngest age ($24$ Jupiter masses and $0.097$ solar radii), and $100\ \text{g/cm}^3$ for those corresponding to the oldest age ($45$ Jupiter masses and $0.084$ solar radii). The brown dwarf has a mass six to twelve times smaller than the star's, but it is two to six times denser: this is an effect of degenerate matter, which in brown dwarfs becomes more compressed the greater the mass. The surface gravity of the two objects, on the other hand, is almost the same, because the star's greater mass is offset by its radius, about three times larger.

In the rows of the table from temperature to density, the lowest values correspond to the youngest age and the highest ones to the oldest age. Later studies have given temperatures between about $560$ and $640\ \text{K}$, depending on the method: the most recent luminosity-based estimate is $586 \pm 35\ \text{K}$ (Burgasser *et al.* 2025).
